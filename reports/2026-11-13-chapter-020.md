## Summary (spoilers)

**S01, Sa3et el ra7a.** At six in the morning the generator's rest hour
stops the fan, and the whole street goes quiet at once. Michael wakes soaked
in Wassim's room. Wassim was Tarek's flatmate and left for Dubai in June;
his world map is still on the wall, with Dubai circled in green. In the
warming fridge are Tarek's mother's trays: kebbe, labne, m2arou2, and warak
3enab in a tin the size of a bucket. She cooked for ten because Tarek said
"a guest" and not "one guest". The family group `El 3ayle` (29 people) wants
photo proof that the guest has eaten. Tarek feeds him cold kebbe at six
(`Kebbe barde 3al sette el sob7 a7la men kebbe so5ne 3al 3asha. Hayda 3elm.`)
and sends the photo: `Akal.`

On the fridge is "the Program": Jbeil, Jeita, the Cedars. Tarek has done it
ten times for cousins, and he performs it as a bus guide with a microphone.
Under every cedar, he says, each visitor says the same sentence: `"Hayde
hiye?"`. Abu 5aled confirms tonight's concrete pour. Tarek apologizes like a
schoolboy (`Ya 3ayb el shoum 3layye`) and starts rescheduling the Program.
Michael stops him by name and asks what his Saturday would have been with no
guest. The answer: sleep until noon, a kishk manqoushe from Abu Sleiman on
the corner, more sleep, and the pour at night. `Hada elli baddi.` Tarek
can't tell whether to be pleased or annoyed. He still promises Jbeil next
Saturday (`Hayde mesh 5yar.`). Then, telling Michael to run the air
conditioning as much as he likes, he says `Ma fi 7sab.`

**S02, El sabbe.** At dusk Michael is on the top slab of a 23-storey tower
above the Corniche. A billboard on the hoarding shows a woman on a balcony
over the words `El ba7er beitak`. Abu 5aled, about 50, from rural Hama, has
poured concrete in Beirut for nineteen years. He names Michael `Shami
mkayyef`, because the boy talks Shami and sells every word. Below them are
the fishermen with rods longer than themselves and the boys jumping from the
rocks at Ain el Mreisseh. The sea goes silver, then orange, then grey like
the concrete.

Tarek prices it. Each flat is 220 m² at 6,000 dollars a metre, and a
20-metre balcony comes to seven years of his salary if he never eats. Then,
in a lull between mixer trucks, he says it for the first time to anyone: a
Riyadh company has offered him two years at four times his pay, with
housing, a car and a ticket home every six months. His answer is due by Sat
Jul 31. Michael says his own dream, `3ish hon, mu zour`. Tarek, who lived
the reverse, tells him what came of it. After a year in Barcelona sharing a
room with Rami, he came back three years ago. Now his mother cooks him kebbe
in a tray, which is guest food: `Rje3et 3a baladi, w sert dayf 3and emme.`
And in Riyadh? `Bi Riyadh bkoun dayf kamen. Bass b ma3ash.`

Michael starts to solve it. Tarek stops him twice, by name: `Ma baddi 7all.
Baddi yeha tebqa 3and 7ada, mesh ma3 7ada.` Tarek asks him to tell no one,
not even Rami, because Rami has his mother's number. Michael swallows the
advice he had ready and promises. Rami texts to ask whether Tarek is still
selling him the country by the metre. Michael's reply is his first cover
story: `Tarek mni7. 3am yeshte8el.` Later Abu 5aled has Michael hold a phone
light while he floats the concrete. That, he says, earns Michael a share in
the building: `Santi. La t2oul lal bawwab.`

**S03, El moteur.** At midnight the breaker drops. Tarek is asleep sitting
up, with one boot off, and sends Michael down to Nayla, behind the shuttered
pressing. Michael lowers the labeled breakers in the flat but misses the
unlabeled one, which still runs a kitchen lamp. The generator is the size of
a car, and its cables climb the walls like a vine. Nayla is 25, in a T-shirt
with a diesel stain, with a ledger and a calculator. She runs her father's
four streets of subscriptions (230 of them) in summer and teaches maths to
seventh graders from September. Her father won't cut off old people who
don't pay; she will.

She lets slip that Tarek went from five amps to ten on Thursday "menshan el
Amerkani" and told him nothing. She learns that Michael "wants to live here"
and asks the only question that matters to her: `Kam ampère?` Then she
prices his dream on the calculator, line by line. A balcony with no sea in
Badaro is 700 a month. Ten amps in summer is 200, state power 20 if it
comes, and the weekly water tank 20. With no AC, five amps is enough, and
`El Corniche bebalash.` Everyone, she says, says "we'll leave" and then does
the sums. She is staying (`Ana ba2ye`). Tarek, in her view, isn't one of the
leavers: a man who pays for ten amps for a guest and says nothing.

She asks if he switched everything off. He says yes. She lays his hand on
TAREK 4 with hers over it (`Shway shway. Mesh b 2awe.`). The forgotten
kitchen lamp lights up on the fourth floor and gives him away. Her hand
stays a second longer than it needs to: `Hayde awwal darse. El tene
bkallef.` Then Dr. Haddad's breaker goes, and she leaves without looking
back. Her number is on Tarek's bill.

Upstairs, Tarek half wakes. They are going up to the Chouf tomorrow, and his
mother will ask if Michael ate: `W eza sa2let 3anne, qella enno ana—`. He is
asleep before he finishes, and Michael doesn't finish it for him. The ending
is new. The guest does the host's arithmetic. Michael takes off Tarek's
other boot, and at the board under Tarek's handwriting he chooses what the
ten amps are for. Up go the fridge, the salon AC (its air falls on the
sleeping host) and Tarek's phone on the charger, because the site calls at
six. Wassim's AC and the water heater stay down. Out loud, quieter the
second time: `Sette ilak. Wa7ad w nos lal berrad. Nos lal marwa7a.` — `W
ana bi 5amse.`

## Honest quality assessment

**Strongest: S02.** The slab above the Corniche is the chapter's real
achievement. The sea is priced by the metre on a building advertised as
`El ba7er beitak`, and Tarek's confession turns Michael's dream inside out
without a speech: the son who came home and became a guest. Michael is not
allowed to fix it, which is the change for a character who has been fixing
things since February. The S03 ending is a genuinely new shape for the
book. It is an act of allocation, the guest doing the host's sums, and the
title earns itself in the last line.

**Weakest: S01.** The Program-on-the-fridge parody runs a little long, and
the scene's mechanics of fridge, trays and phone lean on objects more than
the book's rule likes. Nayla is vivid but arrives with a ready-made "local
truth-teller" function. Her pricing of his dream comes close to a
lesson-giver, and it is rescued by her own stake (`Ana ba2ye`) and by the
lamp catching his lie. The hand on the breaker is the third hand-touch in
four chapters, flagged for the future.

Lebanon is present without postcard shots: heat, generators, prices, the
Corniche from above, a Syrian foreman. The chapter has no profanity, which
fits first meetings and a host, but the summer will need rougher voices.
Barcelona appears once, in Rami's message, and earns its place. Language
naturalness is judged by AI only.
